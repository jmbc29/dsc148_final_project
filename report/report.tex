\begin{abstract}
Win prediction in professional esports is usually framed as binary classification over
game-state features measured at a single fixed timestamp. This snapshot framing discards a basic
piece of information: the \emph{direction} in which the game state is moving. We introduce a
\emph{trajectory-aware} feature set that augments standard 10- and 15-minute differentials with
momentum terms capturing how gold, experience (XP), and kill leads change between those two
checkpoints, and we evaluate it across four model families---Logistic Regression, Gaussian Naive
Bayes, Random Forest, and LightGBM---on 5{,}000 professional matches from the 2024 Oracle Elixir
dataset spanning ten leagues. We additionally define a distinct \emph{comeback} task: among teams
trailing by more than 500 gold at ten minutes, predict which recover and win. Our findings are
reported without spin. After feature engineering, a well-regularized Logistic Regression is the
strongest model on both tasks (win AUC 0.785; comeback AUC 0.734), and gradient boosting does
\emph{not} improve on it---an informative negative result for a literature that increasingly
reaches for complex models. An ablation study shows that objective control, not trajectory, is
the dominant signal: removing objective features costs 0.023 AUC, while removing trajectory
features costs only 0.002. Yet the single most-split feature in LightGBM is gold momentum,
exposing a gap between how heavily a feature is \emph{used} and how much unique signal it
\emph{adds}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Competitive esports has become a rich applied domain for data mining, driven by fine-grained
match logs that capture game state across millions of matches. League of Legends (LoL), developed
by Riot Games, is among the most-played and most-watched titles worldwide, with a professional
ecosystem spanning Korea (LCK), China (LPL), Europe (LEC), North America (LCS), and numerous
regional circuits.

A central problem in this domain is: \emph{given observable game state at an early, fixed
timestamp, what is the probability that a team wins?} Most prior work treats this as a static
classification problem---featurizing the state at 10 or 15 minutes and fitting a classifier on
those snapshot values. This framing ignores the \emph{trajectory} of the game. A team holding a
1{,}000-gold lead that grew from zero at ten minutes is in a very different position from a team
holding a 1{,}000-gold lead that shrank from 3{,}000: the former is snowballing, the latter is
losing control. The two states are indistinguishable to a snapshot model.

We make three contributions. \textbf{(1)} We introduce a trajectory-aware feature set that
extends the standard snapshot representation with momentum terms describing how gold, XP, and kill
differentials evolve between 10 and 15 minutes, evaluated across four model families rather than
tied to one classifier. \textbf{(2)} We define and study a \emph{comeback} task---predicting
recovery for teams that trail at ten minutes---as a separate, harder, class-imbalanced problem.
\textbf{(3)} We report an honest comparative analysis: rather than declaring a complex model the
winner, we show that a regularized linear model is strongest overall after feature engineering,
that trajectory information is heavily used but only marginally irreplaceable, and that objective
control dominates the predictive signal. We view this cautionary result as more useful to
practitioners than a marginal leaderboard win.

\section{Dataset}
\subsection{Source and Scope}
We use the 2024 Oracle Elixir professional match dataset~\cite{oracleselixir}, the standard public
source for professional LoL analytics, which aggregates match data from the Riot Games API across
all major and regional leagues at multiple timestamps. The complete dataset comprises roughly
60{,}000 team- and player-level rows across 5{,}000 unique professional matches. Our
win-prediction analysis operates at the team-game level; we adopt the Blue-side perspective,
yielding 5{,}000 labeled instances. The modeling set can be doubled to 10{,}000 by including
Red-side rows, which we leave as a straightforward extension.

\subsection{Key Statistics}
The data span all ten primary leagues. By match count the largest are LPL (1{,}032 games, 20.6\%),
LCK (1{,}011, 20.2\%), LEC (645, 12.9\%), and LCS (483, 9.7\%), with six regional circuits
contributing the balance (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). Matches run from January to December 2024
across patches 14.1--14.16. Game lengths range from 25 to 60 minutes with a mean of 42.6 minutes,
and the Blue-side win rate is 51.4\%, matching the modest Blue-side advantage reported in prior
work~\cite{maymin2021}. After filtering to complete records, the key features contain no missing
values, so no imputation is required.

\subsection{Exploratory Findings}
Three EDA findings motivate our design. First, gold differential at 15 minutes is the strongest
single linear predictor of outcome ($r=0.40$), followed by kill differential at 15 minutes
($r=0.34$); mid-game state is more predictive than early state, justifying 15-minute features.
Second, outcome is highly conditional on early gold state (Figure~\ref{fig:comeback}): teams
\emph{winning} at ten minutes win 71.2\% of the time, even games resolve near 51.6\%, and teams
\emph{losing} by 500+ gold win only 31.2\%. That comeback rate decays sharply with deficit
size---from 36.0\% for a 500--1000 gold deficit to 6.5\% beyond 2{,}000 gold---establishing the
comeback task as well-defined and nontrivial. Third, objective counts show strongly nonlinear
relationships with win rate, motivating tree-based models alongside linear baselines.

\section{Predictive Tasks}
\subsection{Task 1: Win Prediction}
The primary task is binary classification: given team-level features observable through fifteen
minutes, predict whether the team wins. \textbf{Metrics.} We report Accuracy, F1, and AUC-ROC
under 5-fold stratified cross-validation; AUC-ROC is primary as it is threshold-independent and
standard in prior work. \textbf{Baselines.} Logistic Regression (linear log-odds), Gaussian Naive
Bayes (conditional independence), and Random Forest (nonparametric ensemble) span a spectrum of
modeling assumptions against which our nonlinear candidate, LightGBM, is measured.

\subsection{Task 2: Comeback Classification}
The secondary task restricts to games where Blue trails by more than 500 gold at ten minutes
(1{,}307 games, 26.1\% of the Blue-side sample) and predicts recovery. This is imbalanced
(positive rate 31.2\%) and intentionally harder than Task 1. \textbf{Metrics.} F1 is primary; we
also report AUC-ROC, Precision, and Recall, with class-weight balancing for all models.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Feature Engineering}
We engineer 21 features in six groups (Table~\ref{tab:features}). The \emph{trajectory} group is
the novel element. Gold momentum is
\begin{equation}
  \text{gold\_momentum} = \text{GD}_{15} - \text{GD}_{10},
\end{equation}
positive when a lead grows (snowballing) and negative when it shrinks; XP and kill momentum are
analogous. Interaction terms encode disproportionately decisive joint effects, e.g.
\begin{equation}
  \text{gold\_x\_baron} = \text{GD}_{15}\times(\text{barons}-\text{opp\_barons}),
\end{equation}
capturing that a gold lead with baron control exceeds the value of either alone.

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Engineered feature groups (21 features).}
\label{tab:features}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llc@{}}
\toprule
Group & Features & \# \\
\midrule
Early (10m)  & GD, XPD, CSD, KD at 10m & 4 \\
Mid (15m)    & GD, XPD, CSD, KD at 15m & 4 \\
Trajectory   & $\D$GD, $\D$XPD, $\D$KD (10$\to$15m) & 3 \\
Objectives   & dragon/baron/herald/tower diff, plates, first-obj & 6 \\
Interactions & GD$_{15}\!\times\!$baron, GD$_{15}\!\times\!$dragon & 2 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Models}
We treat all four models as candidates for the same feature set. \textbf{Logistic Regression}
(standardized, $C{=}1.0$) is the linear reference; \textbf{Naive Bayes} a lower bound;
\textbf{Random Forest} (200 trees, depth 8) a strong nonparametric baseline; and
\textbf{LightGBM}~\cite{lightgbm} the gradient-boosted candidate (500 estimators, learning rate
0.05, 31 leaves, depth 6, subsample/colsample 0.8, $\ell_1/\ell_2{=}0.1$, 50-round early
stopping).

\section{Related Work}
Win prediction in MOBA games dates to at least 2014. Yang et al.~\cite{yang2014} showed draft
composition carries significant signal in Dota~2. Semenov et al.~\cite{semenov2016} applied
logistic regression and random forests to MOBA outcome prediction, reporting low-70\% accuracy
from combined features---comparable to our Task 1. Hodge et al.~\cite{hodge2019} used sequence
models on live Dota~2 telemetry, outperforming static baselines. We differ by (1) isolating
trajectory between two checkpoints as explicit, interpretable features rather than learning it
from full sequences; (2) formulating comeback as a distinct task; and (3) reporting a controlled
comparison whose headline is a cautionary result rather than a state-of-the-art claim.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Task 1: Win Prediction}
Table~\ref{tab:task1} and Figure~\ref{fig:models} report 5-fold CV results. The clearest finding
is that \textbf{Logistic Regression is best on every metric} (accuracy 0.711, F1 0.720, AUC
0.785). LightGBM is competitive (AUC 0.771) but does not surpass it; Random Forest trails (0.770)
and Naive Bayes is weakest (0.754). Once trajectory and interaction terms are engineered, the
residual relationship between features and win probability is close to linear, leaving little
nonlinear structure for boosting to exploit.

\subsection{Task 2: Comeback Prediction}
Table~\ref{tab:task2} shows the same ordering: Logistic Regression leads on F1 (0.551) and AUC

Threshold calibration (e.g.\ Platt scaling) is the obvious next step.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
Removing one feature group at a time from a lightweight LightGBM (Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}); its
absolute AUC (0.753) is below the tuned model in Table~\ref{tab:task1}, so relative drops are what
matter. \textbf{Removing Objectives causes by far the largest drop} ($-0.023$ AUC, to 0.730),
confirming objective control dominates. Removing Trajectory costs only $-0.002$ AUC---small but
consistent across folds. Removing Mid (15m) slightly \emph{raises} AUC (to 0.756), revealing
redundancy between the 15-minute snapshot and the trajectory/early features.

\subsection{Feature Importance}
By LightGBM split count (Figure~\ref{fig:importance}), the most-used feature is \emph{gold
momentum}, ahead of XP differential at 15 minutes and gold differential at 10 minutes. The tension
with the ablation result is the paper's most interesting point: trajectory is the feature the
model splits on most, yet removing it barely changes AUC, because momentum is a deterministic
function of the 10- and 15-minute differentials. Split usage measures convenience, not unique
contribution.

\subsection{Patch Sensitivity}
Training on patches 14.1--14.11 and testing on 14.12--14.16, LightGBM reached AUC 0.761, close to
its CV value (0.771), indicating the feature set transfers across patches---unsurprising, since
objective-control mechanics are relatively patch-stable.

\subsection{Case Study}
Consider Blue holding $+500$ gold at ten minutes but a gold momentum of $-800$, arriving at $-300$
at fifteen minutes. A snapshot model rates this near a coin flip; a trajectory-aware model assigns
a lower win probability because negative momentum signals Red is seizing control. The interaction
of \texttt{kd10}, \texttt{gd15}, and \texttt{gold\_momentum} distinguishes ``ahead and fading''
from ``ahead and extending''---the distinction a snapshot discards.

\section{Discussion}
\textbf{Why the linear model wins.} Feature engineering does much of the work a nonlinear model
would otherwise learn: momentum is a hand-computed difference and the interaction terms supply
products a tree would need many splits to approximate. What remains is close to linearly
separable. \textbf{Used vs.\ irreplaceable.} The gold-momentum result cautions against reading
split-based importance as causal contribution; importance and ablation answer different
questions. \textbf{Limitations.} We use team-level aggregates only; player skill, matchups, and
draft synergy are unobserved. We model 5{,}000 Blue-side games; Red-side inclusion would double
the sample. Comeback labels use a 500-gold/ten-minute threshold; alternatives may differ.
\textbf{Broader use.} Win-probability models support broadcast overlays, coaching tools, and draft
simulators; the comeback classifier surfaces high-leverage moments.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented a trajectory-aware feature set for win and comeback prediction in professional League
of Legends, evaluated across four model families on 5{,}000 Oracle Elixir 2024 matches. Reported
without spin: a regularized Logistic Regression is strongest on both tasks, gradient boosting
offers no improvement once trajectory and interaction features are engineered explicitly, and
objective control---not trajectory---is the dominant signal. Trajectory features are nonetheless
the most heavily used by the boosted model, a discrepancy we trace to redundancy with the raw
differentials. The system is deployed as an interactive dashboard. In this domain, careful
features and a simple model beat a complex model on autopilot.
